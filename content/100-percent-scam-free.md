## Is it a scam?

The Federal Trade Commission suggests four signs that something is a scam, all starting
with the letter 'P' for easier recall:

1. **Pretend** - Scammers pretend to be from an organization you know, whether that is a
   government agency or UPS or Amazon. Sometimes they'll pretend to be someone that
   knows one of your family members.
2. **Problem** or **Prize** - Scammers will tell you that there's some kind of problem,
   ranging from the mundane "There's something wrong with your order" or "We can't
   deliver your package" to saying you're in trouble with the police. They might also
   say that you've won something.
3. **Pressure** - Scammers need to you act immediately, so they'll put on the pressure.
   They'll make it sound like the problem is an emergency, or that you'll lose the prize
   if you don't act right now.
4. **Pay** - Scammers will ask you to pay in a way that is unconventional and hard to
   trace, like with gift cards, money transfers, cryptocurrency, etc.

You can read the [FTC's description of these signs
here](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-avoid-scam).

## A story

A neighbor of mine that I sometimes help with computer problems told me a story a little
while back. He got a call telling him that someone had hacked his computer and were
using it to serve up child pornography on the Internet. The caller said he was sure my
neighbor wasn't to blame for the content, but being a federal crime, of course law
enforcement had been notified. Not to worry, though, the caller would help him
straighten things out if my neighbor would just install a little program on his
computer... He was freaking out, so was grateful to have someone "helping" him. In fact,
even later when it was apparent that he had been scammed, my neighbor commented that he
just felt relief that the FBI wasn't going to be knocking on his door at any moment.

There are some details of the situation that I don't know, but you can see some of the
warning signs. Often such callers will **pretend** that they're from Microsoft or
another well-known technology company. In this case, my neighbor had a **problem** so
big that being scammed was the least of his worries. **Pressure** was applied by saying
that law enforcement had been notified and would be contacting him. I'm not sure if any
kind of **payment** took place, but the scammer had definitely taken a look at my
neighbor's bank account. He speculated that the scammer saw so little money that it
wasn't worth the trouble.

That, in itself is a bit of a myth: Scammers won't come after me because I'm poor!

The National Council on Aging has a [scam education
course](https://www.ncoa.org/article/seniors-against-scams-financial-education) and the
target audience is actually low- and moderate-income people. I think the reason for that
is because falling victim to fraud is more devastating to such people. They do also
point out that one in five victims of a financial scam in the U.S. is aged 60+, so
realize that you're a target if you're older.

## I'm not an idiot

In the case of my neighbor, it is easy say, "What were you thinking?" But that's
ignoring the psychology of the situation. When you're afraid and under pressure you
don't think as clearly -- so scammers try to keep you from taking a step back and
evaluating things.

## More example scams

Here are a few examples of common scams:

1. **Social Security phone** - Someone pretending to be from the SSA will call saying
   that your SSN is going to be canceled for whatever reason, and can you please provide
   the following missing information... which they will then use to steal your identity.
   They might even fake the caller ID so it looks like the call is really coming from
   a government agency.
2. **IRS email or text** - You'll get a message about your refund, telling you to click
   a link or fill out an online form to claim it.
3. **Home repair** - This is often an in-person scam, where someone will say they
   noticed some work that needs to be done on your home. They'll quote a reasonable
   price but insist on a deposit (or the entire amount) up-front and will either not do
   the work or do only the most superficial job. This happens a lot if there is some
   kind of natural disaster where lots of people are needing work done.
3. **Grandparent scam** - "Hi Grandma! Guess who this is!" Hmm... sounds kind of like
   Johnny. There will then be a story about how they're in trouble and need some money
   right away and ask you to send it. "And please don't tell my parents, Grandma! They'd
   kill me if they found out!"

There are so many! The FTC has [a huge list you can look
through](https://consumer.ftc.gov/all-scams). They also have an email newsletter if you
want to get your information in smaller doses. You can sign up for those at
[https://ftc.gov/ConsumerAlerts](https://ftc.gov/ConsumerAlerts).

## Tips

Finally, here are some tips to avoid scams:

1. Don't answer phone calls from unknown numbers. If it is important they'll leave a
   message.
2. Don't click on links in emails or texts unless you're sure they're something you're
   expecting and from a trusted source. Even then, I often navigate to the site myself
   rather than click links.
3. Get any offers or arrangements in writing, and make sure you have full contact
   information.
4. Don't do business with people that just knock on your door.
5. Set up a secret "password" with your loved ones so you can verify their identity in
   case someone pretends to be them.
6. Don't give out any personal information unless you initiated the call/exchange
   yourself.
7. Set up 2FA (Two-Factor Authentication) on accounts, especially things like email and
   financial institutions.
8. Set up alerts for financial accounts, like for purchases when your credit card was
   not physically presented, or for expenses over a certain dollar amount.
